# Game Conception

Co-create a game the user wants to inhabit and play. Keep its emotional promise connected to what the player repeatedly does, perceives and chooses.

Use the `brainstorming` skill when it is among your available skills for the collaborative stance and its Vibestorming method. Otherwise offer concrete contrasting attempts, invite warm/cold reactions, preserve what resonates and change what misses. The user decides resonance; design reasoning tests whether the proposed play can deliver it.

## Start with something playable

Respond to the seed itself: an image, a mechanic, a remembered feeling, a world or a messy fragment. Contribute a specific possibility immediately. Ask only the question that would most change the next creative move, using the conversation's language.

When the direction is open, offer two or three genuinely different short scenes. Each gives the player an action and a consequence, alongside sensory detail. Vary the fantasy or kind of play, so choosing among them reveals more than a preferred coat of paint. When the user already has a direction, develop it directly.

Keep exploration conversational. Read [creative-moves.md](references/creative-moves.md) when the user is stuck, cannot articulate the vibe, or when proposals keep converging on familiar genre formulas.

## Find the vibe through play

Treat mood words as hypotheses. Make “lonely,” “cozy,” “brutal” or “dreamlike” concrete through space, camera distance, sound, movement, pace and consequences. Distinguish atmosphere (what surrounds the player), player fantasy (who they get to be), and game feel (how their actions respond).

For warm/cold feedback, retain the specific image, phrase or interaction that landed. Change one meaningful dimension in the next attempt; if the whole direction is cold, branch more widely. A rejection without an explanation is enough to try another angle. Keep proposed interpretations separate from confirmed preferences, and leave mixed feelings mixed until the user resolves them.

Translate a promising vibe into a causal chain:

> Intended feeling → repeated action or choice → system response → sensory feedback → reason to continue.

For example, protective tenderness might mean shielding a small creature from wind: moving close changes its gait and sound; taking an exposed shortcut saves time but unsettles it. That interaction gives the feeling something to live in. It remains a hypothesis until played.

When references to existing games, films, places or music help, identify the exact quality being borrowed and the intended departure. Keep comparisons grounded in known details; verify uncertain factual claims when they matter. Original imagined references and scenes can stay speculative.

## Discover the game inside the scene

Once a direction has shape, walk through roughly a minute of actual play: what the player notices, attempts, chooses, receives as feedback, and does next. Include a meaningful alternative or opportunity for expression. Discovery, care, traversal and creation can carry play as well as conflict.

From that scene, extract only the design needed to explain it:

- **Player verbs and agency:** what the player controls, and what makes different actions matter.
- **Core loop:** how actions change the situation and lead into another interesting action. Explain what varies on repetition.
- **Rhythm:** the relation between momentary feedback, a session's arc and longer change, where the concept needs those timescales.
- **Tension and recovery:** what uncertainty, cost or friction serves the intended feeling, and how the player can recover or stop.
- **Identity:** the interaction or relationship that makes this worth exploring beyond its genre label.

Name contradictions concretely. If a proposed timer undermines unhurried curiosity, explain the trade-off and offer a version that preserves urgency differently. Expand early; test the strongest idea once there is something to test. Protect the unusual part while pruning features that compete with it.

## Ground it in PC and console play

Let the concept determine which constraints matter. Ask about team, resources and target hardware when they change the concept or when the user wants feasibility; label assumptions in the meantime.

Consider controller and mouse/keyboard fit, camera and aiming demands, action readability at a television distance, text density, session length and save/return behavior. For multiplayer, establish local or online play and what players actually do together. Surface accessibility tensions in the central interaction, such as reliance on color, sound alone or rapid repeated inputs, with alternatives that preserve its feeling.

Keep platform-specific certification, performance targets and engine selection for a technical planning pass unless they already constrain the idea. Treat production effort as a reasoned uncertainty. Scope down by preserving the defining interaction and cutting content breadth or secondary systems.

## Crystallize when invited

Continue exploring while the user is still finding the game. When they ask for a concept or choose a direction, use [concept-brief.md](references/concept-brief.md) to produce a compact brief at the requested depth. If they asked for a deliverable from the outset, draft it immediately with visible assumptions.

Completion means the reader can picture a playable moment, trace the vibe to design choices, and identify the next uncertainty to test. Record confirmed choices separately from suggestions and open questions. A prototype proposal states what behavior would support or weaken the concept; a written pitch cannot prove fun.

For production planning, use the `project-conception` skill when it is among your available skills; otherwise summarize scope, dependencies and the next decision. Carry the emotional promise and defining interaction into the handoff.
