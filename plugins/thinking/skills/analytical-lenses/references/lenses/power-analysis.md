# Power Analysis

## What this lens sees

The distribution of capacity to *decide*, *benefit*, *pay*, and *exit*. Power analysis treats every situation as having a structure of asymmetric capacities and asks who holds what – often revealing that the formal description of a situation hides its actual mechanics.

## Key questions

- Who has the formal authority to decide?
- Who has the informal authority – the ability to block, delay, or shape what gets decided?
- Who benefits if this proceeds as planned? Who pays the cost?
- Who is dependent on whom? Who could walk away without consequence, and who could not?
- Where is information asymmetric – who knows things the others do not?
- What are the exit costs for each party? (High exit costs = low actual freedom regardless of formal status.)
- What kinds of power are operating – economic, legal, social, informational, infrastructural, normative?

## Characteristic moves

1. Distinguish *formal* power (titles, contracts, votes) from *operative* power (who actually shapes outcomes).
2. Map the dependencies: who needs whom to do what.
3. Ask whether the situation looks different to the most powerful party vs. the least.
4. Identify chokepoints – places where one party's action determines what is possible for all others.

## Known blind spots

Power analysis can flatten everything into "who has power over whom" and miss situations where actors are constrained by *no one in particular* (structural forces, market dynamics, technological lock-in). It also tends to read motives into structural advantage where none may exist – a party benefiting from an arrangement did not necessarily design it. Pair with `externalities` or `scale-shifts` to avoid this.

## Output format

A short prose paragraph identifying the dominant power structure, followed by 3–5 bullet observations on specific asymmetries that matter for the situation.
