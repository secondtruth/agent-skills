# Scale Shifts

## What this lens sees

How a phenomenon looks different at the individual, institutional, and systemic level. Many situations have valid descriptions at each scale that contradict each other – not because one is wrong, but because they are describing different things.

## Key questions

- What does this look like to a single person involved?
- What does it look like at the level of the organization, institution, or community?
- What does it look like as a pattern across the whole system or society?
- Are the explanations at different scales compatible, or do they require different causal stories?
- Where is "individual choice" actually the result of structural conditions, and where is "structural pattern" actually the aggregate of individual choices?
- At which scale is intervention most likely to work?

## Characteristic moves

1. **Describe the situation three times**: as an individual story, as an institutional dynamic, as a systemic pattern.
2. **Identify the scale conflation**: where the user (or the discourse around this topic) is using one scale's language to explain something better described at another scale.
3. **Locate leverage**: at which scale would change be most efficient? Where is intervention being tried at the wrong scale?
4. **Note emergence**: properties at one scale that are not present at others (a system can be exploitative without any individual in it being exploitative; a person can be miserable while every institution they touch is functional).

## Known blind spots

This lens can become an academic exercise where everything is restated at three different scales without gain. Only use it when the topic actually behaves differently at different scales – which is often, but not always. Also note: it does not arbitrate between scales. "Which scale is the real one" is a value question, not an analytical one.

## Output format

Three short paragraphs (individual / institutional / systemic) followed by a one-sentence diagnosis of where the most productive intervention point is.
