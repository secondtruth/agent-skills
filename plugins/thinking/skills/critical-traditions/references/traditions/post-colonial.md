# Post-Colonial Theory

## What this tradition sees

The cultural, psychological, and discursive aftermath of colonialism – how colonial structures of representation, identity, and knowledge persist after formal political independence, and how the colonized subject and colonizer alike are constituted by the colonial encounter. Post-colonial theory is text- and discourse-oriented in a way that decolonial theory (with which it overlaps) is not.

## Core concepts

- **Orientalism** (Said) – the discursive system through which "the East" was constructed by the West as a coherent, knowable, and inferior other; the way knowledge production served imperial power
- **Hybridity** (Bhabha) – the in-between cultural space produced by colonial encounter; not pure mixing but a destabilization of the categories themselves
- **Mimicry** (Bhabha) – the colonial demand that the colonized resemble the colonizer "almost the same but not quite", producing a destabilizing ambivalence
- **The subaltern** (Spivak, after Gramsci) – those positioned outside the structures of representation and able to "speak" only through them; the political-epistemological problem of speaking *for* the subaltern
- **Strategic essentialism** (Spivak) – the tactical use of essentialist categories for political mobilization, while acknowledging their constructed status

## Key questions

- How is the discourse around this topic shaping who can speak, in what terms, and with what authority?
- What identities and categories that appear natural in the situation were actually produced through colonial encounter?
- Whose representations are dominant, and who is being represented vs. representing themselves?
- Where does "translation" between cultural contexts smuggle in the values of one context as universal?

## Characteristic moves

1. Treat discourse as a site of power, not merely a description of it.
2. Locate the cultural / textual / representational dimension of political situations.
3. Resist the urge to recover an "authentic" pre-colonial identity – attend instead to hybridity, ambivalence, and reconstruction.
4. Hold attention to the position of the analyst – who is speaking, from where, with what authority and what blind spots.

## Internal contestation

Major tension with decolonial theory (Latin American school): postcolonial theory is criticized as too text-bound, too located in the Western academy, too focused on Anglophone South Asia. Critique from materialist Marxism (Aijaz Ahmad, Vivek Chibber): postcolonialism overstates cultural specificity and underplays universal capitalist dynamics. Internal debate over whether the "post" is misleading – critics argue colonialism is not over, just transformed.

## Known weaknesses

Strongly developed for cultural-textual analysis, less so for institutional and economic questions. Often Anglophone-Indian-centric in its canon. Can produce extremely dense theoretical prose that is itself accused of reproducing the elite-academic exclusions it critiques. Sometimes the focus on discursive critique displaces strategic political questions.

## Primary sources

- Edward Said, "Orientalism" (1978) – the foundational text
- Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak, "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988); "A Critique of Postcolonial Reason" (1999)
- Homi K. Bhabha, "The Location of Culture" (1994)
- Frantz Fanon, "Black Skin, White Masks" (1952); "The Wretched of the Earth" (1961) – predecessor for both postcolonial and decolonial traditions
- Aijaz Ahmad, "In Theory: Classes, Nations, Literatures" (1992) – Marxist counter-critique

## Especially useful for

Literary and cultural analysis, identity formation in cross-cultural contexts, representation in media and academia, history of ideas, situations where the question is how meaning is produced and contested.

## Less useful for

Material-economic analysis where decolonial or Marxist critique cuts more directly. Engineering, scientific, or technical questions where the textual-discursive frame strains to find purchase.
