# Deontological Ethics

## What this tradition sees

Morality as defined by *duties* and *rights* that hold regardless of consequences. Some acts are wrong (or required) in themselves – treating a person as a mere means is wrong even if it would produce better outcomes; honoring a promise binds even when breaking it would be more useful. Deontology centers the *rational structure* of moral obligation and the *dignity* of persons as the source of moral law.

## Core concepts

- **Duty (Pflicht)** – moral obligation that holds for its own sake, not for the sake of its consequences
- **Categorical imperative** (Kant) – act only on maxims you could will to be universal law; treat humanity, in yourself and others, never merely as means but always also as ends
- **Autonomy** – the capacity to give moral law to oneself; the rational ground of human dignity
- **Persons vs. things** – the foundational distinction; persons have dignity (Würde), things have price (Preis)
- **Rights** – correlates of duties; what is owed to a person as a matter of moral entitlement, not benevolence
- **The kingdom of ends** – the ideal community of rational beings each treated as an end in themselves; a regulative principle for moral and political life
- **Original position / veil of ignorance** (Rawls) – the contractualist thought experiment for deriving principles of justice that any rational person would accept

## Key questions

- Is anyone in this situation being treated merely as a means?
- What duties bind regardless of how the calculation comes out?
- What rights are being respected, violated, or ignored?
- Could the principle of action here be willed as universal – or does it depend on others not following it?
- What does respect for autonomy require here, including the autonomy of those whose preferences we disagree with?

## Characteristic moves

1. Identify the rights at stake and the duties they impose.
2. Run the universalization test: what if this principle were applied generally?
3. Distinguish acts that are *wrong in themselves* from acts that are *bad in consequence*; protect the former from being overridden by the latter.
4. Hold the line on persons-as-ends even when the consequentialist case for treating them as means is strong.

## Internal contestation

The tradition contains substantially different programs:
- **Kantian** – built on the categorical imperative and the dignity of rational nature
- **Rawlsian / contractualism** – derives principles from what rational agents would agree to under fair conditions
- **Scanlonian contractualism** – "what we owe to each other"; principles no one could reasonably reject
- **Threshold deontology** – consequences matter, but only above a threshold of moral catastrophe
- **Natural rights theory** – rights as moral facts independent of social construction

Disagreement over: how to handle conflicts between duties; whether the duty / right structure is ultimately derivable or simply foundational; the relationship to political and legal institutions; how to handle the "demandingness" problem (consequentialism asks too much; deontology asks too little?).

## Known weaknesses

The classic objection: deontology can produce verdicts that strike most people as monstrous when followed strictly (the murderer-at-the-door case in Kant). Threshold deontology mitigates this but at the cost of theoretical cleanness. The universalization test is open to formulating maxims at convenient levels of specificity. Difficulty handling supererogatory acts (going beyond duty). Kant's own writings include substantial racism that the tradition has had to reckon with.

## Primary sources

- Immanuel Kant, "Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals" (1785); "Critique of Practical Reason" (1788); "Metaphysics of Morals" (1797)
- John Rawls, "A Theory of Justice" (1971); "Political Liberalism" (1993)
- Thomas Scanlon, "What We Owe to Each Other" (1998)
- Christine Korsgaard, "The Sources of Normativity" (1996)
- Frances Kamm, "Intricate Ethics" (2007)

## Especially useful for

Rights questions, law and constitutional principles, medical ethics, contexts where consequentialist reasoning would license violating individuals for aggregate benefit, professional ethics with clear duty structure (lawyers, doctors), questions about respect and dignity.

## Less useful for

Policy choice under uncertainty and tradeoffs where consequentialist accounting cannot be avoided; situations where the rights framework produces gridlock and a decision is needed; novel situations where the duty structure is genuinely unclear.
