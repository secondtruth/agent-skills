# Marxist Critique

## What this tradition sees

Capitalism as a historically specific social formation with internal contradictions, organized around the exploitation of labor and the accumulation of capital, generating both material productivity and recurring crisis. Marxism reads social phenomena through their place in the mode of production: who works for whom, who owns what, what is being produced and on whose terms.

## Core concepts

- **Mode of production** – the historically specific way a society organizes production, encompassing forces (technology, labor) and relations (ownership, class) of production
- **Class** – defined by relationship to means of production, not by income, identity, or culture
- **Surplus value** / **exploitation** – the structural difference between the value produced by labor and what labor is paid; the source of profit
- **Commodity fetishism** – the appearance of social relations between people as relations between things
- **Primitive accumulation / accumulation by dispossession** – the originary and ongoing violence through which assets, lands, and commons are converted into capital
- **Crisis tendencies** – the recurring contradictions (overproduction, falling rate of profit, ecological limits) that capitalism generates and must displace
- **Ideology** – the systematic mystification of social relations that serves the reproduction of existing class power

## Key questions

- Whose labor produces the value in this situation? Who appropriates it?
- What forms of dispossession – of land, time, attention, data, social bonds – are at work?
- How does the situation reproduce or contest the prevailing class relations?
- What contradictions are present that the situation cannot resolve within its current structure?
- What appears as a "natural" feature of the situation that is actually a historically specific relation?

## Characteristic moves

1. Identify the mode of production at work and the class relations it implies.
2. Read surface phenomena (prices, conflicts, cultural forms) as expressions of underlying material relations.
3. Trace how capital is being accumulated in the situation and at whose expense.
4. Distinguish between contradictions that the system can manage (by displacement) and those that point beyond it.

## Internal contestation

Marxism is not a single tradition but a constellation of substantially disagreeing schools:
- **Classical / orthodox Marxism**: the labor theory of value, base/superstructure, revolutionary politics
- **Western Marxism** (Lukács, Gramsci, Frankfurt School): cultural, ideological, and philosophical extension
- **Analytical Marxism** (G.A. Cohen, John Roemer): rigorous reconstruction in mainstream analytical terms; abandons labor theory of value
- **Autonomist Marxism** (Negri, Tronti): centers the agency of the working class as the motor of capitalist development
- **Ecological Marxism / metabolic rift** (John Bellamy Foster, Saito): reads Marx's mature work as fundamentally ecological
- **Feminist Marxism** – overlaps materialist feminism
- **Critique of value** (Wertkritik) – German current that critiques both the labor theory of value and labor-centric politics

These schools genuinely disagree on the labor theory of value, on revolutionary strategy, on the relation to identity politics, on ecology, and on the post-1989 prospects of the tradition.

## Known weaknesses

Track record of political application is mixed at best – the gap between Marxist theory and the regimes that claimed it is part of the tradition's reckoning. Eurocentric in its classical formulations; needed decolonial and feminist correction. The labor theory of value is contested even among sympathetic economists. Risk of treating non-economic structures (gender, race, ecology) as merely derivative of class; subsequent traditions have substantially corrected this.

## Primary sources

- Karl Marx, "Capital" Vol. I (1867); "Grundrisse" (1857–58 notebooks); "Critique of the Gotha Programme" (1875)
- Marx & Engels, "The German Ideology" (1845/6); "The Communist Manifesto" (1848)
- Rosa Luxemburg, "The Accumulation of Capital" (1913)
- Antonio Gramsci, "Prison Notebooks" (1929–1935)
- David Harvey, "A Companion to Marx's Capital" (2010); "The Limits to Capital" (1982)
- Kohei Saito, "Marx in the Anthropocene" (2023) – ecological reconstruction

## Especially useful for

Political-economic analysis, labor and capital questions, crisis dynamics, structural reading of policy and institutions, technology under capitalism, situations where surface explanations (individual choice, cultural difference, accident) obscure structural relations.

## Less useful for

Topics where class relations are not the dominant axis and forcing them in produces reductive analysis. Modern Marxism in its better forms knows this and pairs with feminist, decolonial, and ecological traditions rather than competing with them.
