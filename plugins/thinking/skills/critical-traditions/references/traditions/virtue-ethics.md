# Virtue Ethics

## What this tradition sees

Ethical life as the cultivation of *character* and the pursuit of human flourishing (eudaimonia), rather than as the application of rules or the maximization of outcomes. The central question is not "what should I do?" but "what kind of person should I become?", and good action follows from good character formed through practice and habituation.

## Core concepts

- **Virtue (aretē)** – a stable disposition to act, feel, and perceive in accordance with what is excellent; not a single act but a settled character trait
- **Eudaimonia** – flourishing, a complete and well-lived life; not pleasure or contentment but the full activity of the human capacities
- **Phronesis** – practical wisdom; the capacity to perceive the right action in particular situations, not derivable from general rules
- **The mean (mesotēs)** – virtue lies between excesses; courage between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between meanness and prodigality
- **Habituation** – virtue is acquired through practice; you become courageous by acting courageously, not by reading about courage
- **Telos** – the characteristic end or function of a being; for humans, the exercise of reason in virtuous activity

## Key questions

- What character traits are being developed or eroded by this situation?
- What would a person of practical wisdom do here – not what would a rule prescribe?
- What is the proper function (telos) of the practice / institution / role under analysis, and is it being well exercised?
- Where is the situation pulling people toward extremes that a virtuous person would moderate?
- What forms of flourishing are made possible or foreclosed?

## Characteristic moves

1. Reframe the question from "what action" to "what character".
2. Identify the relevant virtues and their corresponding vices (excess and deficiency).
3. Locate the *practical* wisdom required – the perception of particulars, not the application of universals.
4. Examine the social practices and institutions in which character is formed – virtue ethics is communitarian in this regard, against atomistic ethical frameworks.

## Internal contestation

Three substantial currents disagree on important points:
- **Aristotelian** – the classical tradition, with its commitments to natural teleology and a substantive account of human nature
- **Neo-Aristotelian / MacIntyrean** – revival emphasizing the social and historical embeddedness of virtues; sharper critique of liberal modernity
- **Capabilities approach** (Nussbaum, Sen) – derives from Aristotle but engages with pluralism, liberal politics, and feminist concerns in ways MacIntyre rejects

Disagreement over: whether virtue ethics requires a substantive theory of human nature; how the tradition handles pluralism and disagreement about the good; the relationship to political institutions; whether classical virtue lists need updating.

## Known weaknesses

The question "who counts as a virtuous person" can become circular. Virtue ethics is weakest at giving guidance in situations of acute conflict or systemic injustice where character-formation has been distorted upstream. Critics charge that it offers no clear decision procedure where rules-based or consequentialist approaches do. Risk of cultural conservatism – treating the virtues of a specific community as universal. The Aristotelian inheritance includes substantial sexism and slavery-tolerance that the tradition must work to repudiate rather than smuggle.

## Primary sources

- Aristotle, "Nicomachean Ethics"; "Politics"
- Alasdair MacIntyre, "After Virtue" (1981); "Dependent Rational Animals" (1999)
- Philippa Foot, "Natural Goodness" (2001); "Virtues and Vices" (1978)
- Martha Nussbaum, "Women and Human Development" (2000); "Creating Capabilities" (2011)
- Rosalind Hursthouse, "On Virtue Ethics" (1999)

## Especially useful for

Personal ethical formation, professional ethics (where character of practitioners matters more than rules), questions about institutions and practices and what they make of the people in them, education, the ethics of long-term projects and relationships, situations where rules-based ethics gives the right answer but for the wrong reasons.

## Less useful for

Acute conflicts where what is needed is a decision procedure, not a character development plan. Situations where the social conditions for virtue-formation are themselves the problem (then political-economic critique is upstream).
