# Decolonial Critique

## What this tradition sees

Modernity and coloniality as two faces of the same historical process – there is no modernity without colonial subjugation, and the conceptual structures of contemporary knowledge (universalism, progress, development, even the human/nature divide) carry that origin. Decoloniality is distinct from postcolonialism: it focuses on the persistence of colonial power structures (the "coloniality of power") long after formal independence, and on epistemic disobedience rather than primarily textual critique.

## Core concepts

- **Coloniality of power** (Quijano) – the racialized, gendered, economic global order established by Spanish/Portuguese colonialism and reproduced in subsequent forms; survived formal decolonization
- **Coloniality of knowledge** – the way modern Western epistemology has positioned itself as universal while particularizing all other knowledges; geopolitics of knowledge
- **The modern/colonial world-system** – the analytical insistence that modernity must be theorized as inherently colonial, not as a separable European phenomenon
- **Pluriversality** (vs. universality) – many worlds coexisting, against the modern-colonial project of universalizing one
- **Epistemic disobedience / delinking** (Mignolo) – the strategic refusal to argue within the imposed conceptual framework

## Key questions

- What is being treated as universal that is actually a particular knowledge with colonial provenance?
- Whose knowledge counts in this situation, and whose has been systematically delegitimized?
- Where does the analytical frame itself reproduce colonial categories (development, modernization, "the West and the rest")?
- What would the situation look like told from the standpoint of the historically colonized?

## Characteristic moves

1. Locate the historical origin of the categories being used – they often emerge from specific colonial contexts and then claim universality.
2. Refuse the universal/particular trap: any "view from nowhere" is a view from somewhere disguised.
3. Foreground subjugated knowledges (Indigenous, peasant, feminized, racialized) without romanticizing them.
4. Distinguish *anti-colonial* (against direct colonial rule) from *de-colonial* (against the persistence of colonial structures and concepts after formal independence).

## Internal contestation

Significant debate over the relationship between the Latin American decolonial school (Quijano, Dussel, Mignolo, Maldonado-Torres) and postcolonial theory (Said, Spivak, Bhabha) – overlapping concerns but different lineages, geographies, and emphases. Debate over how decoloniality engages with class analysis (some say insufficiently, others say it transforms it). Tension over whether decoloniality is best understood as a movement, a research program, or an ethical orientation – and whether it has been institutionalized to its own detriment.

## Known weaknesses

Sometimes accused of romanticizing pre-colonial conditions or essentializing "Indigenous knowledge" as a monolithic category. The terminological apparatus can become baroque and self-referential. The political program is more developed as critique than as construction – proposals for institutional alternatives are less worked out than the analysis of what is wrong. Tendency to treat all critique of decoloniality as itself colonial, which forecloses argument.

## Primary sources

- Aníbal Quijano, "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America" (2000)
- Walter Mignolo, "The Darker Side of Western Modernity" (2011); "Local Histories/Global Designs" (2000)
- Enrique Dussel, "The Invention of the Americas" (1995); "Philosophy of Liberation" (1977)
- Nelson Maldonado-Torres, "On the Coloniality of Being" (2007)
- Sylvia Wynter, "Unsettling the Coloniality of Being/Power/Truth/Freedom" (2003)

## Especially useful for

Development questions, international relations, knowledge production and academia, intellectual history, situations where Western universalism is doing unexamined analytical work, environmental questions seen through Global South perspective, technology transfer debates.

## Less useful for

Topics with no significant cross-cultural or historical-colonial dimension. Strained applications (e.g., decolonial reading of an internal corporate restructuring with no relevant cross-cultural axis) produce noise.
