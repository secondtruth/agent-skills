# Ecofeminism

## What this tradition sees

A structural parallel between the domination of women and the domination of nature – both rooted in a logic of mastery, dualism, and devaluation of what is coded as embodied, dependent, or "natural". Ecofeminism reads ecological crisis and gender injustice as expressions of the same underlying pattern, and care – of bodies, of ecosystems, of dependencies – as the alternative organizing principle.

## Core concepts

- **Logic of domination** (Plumwood) – the conceptual structure (dualism, hierarchy, instrumentalism, denial of dependency) that licenses the exploitation of both women and nature
- **Reproductive economy** – the care work, ecological replenishment, and intergenerational sustenance that the productive economy depends on but does not see
- **Nature/culture dualism** – the founding distinction that this tradition treats as a target rather than a given
- **Subsistence perspective** (Mies) – the standpoint of those whose work sustains life directly, as a critical position outside the growth logic
- **Ecological debt** – the cumulative damage done by extractive economies, owed disproportionately by the Global North to the Global South and to future generations

## Key questions

- What forms of dependency are being denied in this situation? (Dependencies on land, on care, on ecosystem services, on the labor of others.)
- Where is "nature" being treated as a resource and "the feminine" as a sphere – are these moves linked?
- Who or what is doing reproductive / regenerative work that is invisible in the dominant accounting?
- What would a care-centered rather than mastery-centered approach to this situation look like?

## Characteristic moves

1. Identify the dualism at work: productive/reproductive, public/private, culture/nature, mind/body, rational/emotional.
2. Show how the dualism is doing political work – which side is valued, which is exploited.
3. Center the perspective of those positioned on the devalued side.
4. Propose alternatives grounded in care, sufficiency, regeneration rather than growth and mastery.

## Internal contestation

Sharp split between essentialist ecofeminism (women as closer to nature by biological or spiritual fact) and constructionist ecofeminism (the connection is socially produced, not natural). The essentialist current is now largely abandoned in academic ecofeminism but persists in popular versions. Active debate over how ecofeminism integrates decolonial and Indigenous perspectives, and whether degrowth fully incorporates ecofeminist insights or co-opts them.

## Known weaknesses

The essentialist legacy still hangs over the tradition. Some formulations universalize "women's experience of nature" in ways that are Western, rural, and class-specific. Practical translation to large-scale policy is weak – the strongest applications are in subsistence economies and grassroots movements; less developed for industrial-system reform.

## Primary sources

- Carolyn Merchant, "The Death of Nature" (1980)
- Vandana Shiva, "Staying Alive" (1989)
- Maria Mies & Vandana Shiva, "Ecofeminism" (1993)
- Val Plumwood, "Feminism and the Mastery of Nature" (1993)
- Ariel Salleh, "Ecofeminism as Politics" (1997)

## Especially useful for

Agriculture and food systems, care work and reproductive labor, climate justice, subsistence economies, body politics, anything where the productive/reproductive boundary or the human/nature dualism is doing structural work.

## Less useful for

Topics where the parallelism between gender and nature does not actually obtain in the situation – forcing it produces strained metaphor rather than analysis.
