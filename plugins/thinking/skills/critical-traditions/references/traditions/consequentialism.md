# Consequentialism / Utilitarianism

## What this tradition sees

The moral worth of an action as determined entirely by its consequences – specifically, by the good (variously specified: pleasure, preference satisfaction, well-being, welfare) it produces and the bad it averts. The right action is the one that produces the best overall outcome. The tradition is committed to *impartiality*: the well-being of any person (often: any sentient being) counts equally.

## Core concepts

- **Utility / welfare** – the good to be maximized; classically pleasure or happiness, in contemporary forms often preference-satisfaction or objective well-being
- **Impartiality** – each affected being's welfare counts the same; no privileging of self, kin, or compatriots in the calculation
- **Act- vs. rule-consequentialism** – evaluate individual acts directly by their consequences, or evaluate rules (and then act on them) by the consequences of generally following them
- **Aggregation** – the move that allows summing welfare across persons; central to the tradition and the locus of its most fundamental critiques
- **Marginal utility** – the basis for redistributive intuitions: a unit of welfare provided to someone with little produces more than the same unit provided to someone with much
- **Expected utility** – under uncertainty, maximize the expectation; rationally tractable but assumes intersubjective comparisons of welfare
- **Effective altruism** – contemporary descendant: applying consequentialist reasoning to questions of how to do the most good with limited resources

## Key questions

- What are the actual consequences of this action / policy / institution, for whom, with what probability?
- Are there interests being underweighted because they are distant in space, time, or species?
- What does the marginal-utility analysis say about where resources should go?
- Is the comparison being run honestly, or is "the consequences" being narrowed to those convenient to count?

## Characteristic moves

1. Specify the welfare function: what counts as good, for whom, with what weighting.
2. Estimate the consequences as best as possible, including uncertainty.
3. Aggregate across affected parties and across time.
4. Compare to alternatives – including the counterfactual baseline of doing nothing or doing otherwise.
5. Defend the impartialist baseline against intuitions that favor partial interests.

## Internal contestation

The tradition has substantial internal variants:
- **Classical utilitarianism** (Bentham, Mill) – pleasure as the ultimate good, hedonic calculus
- **Preference utilitarianism** (Hare, early Singer) – preference satisfaction as the good
- **Welfare consequentialism** – well-being more broadly, often objective list theories
- **Rule consequentialism** (Brad Hooker) – evaluate by consequences of rules generally followed
- **Negative utilitarianism** – prioritize reduction of suffering over maximization of welfare
- **Effective altruism** – applied consequentialism with focus on cause-prioritization and high-impact giving
- **Longtermism** – the EA-adjacent claim that the long-run future dominates moral calculation given the number of beings potentially affected

Major internal disagreements: what counts as welfare; how to handle uncertainty; whether non-human animals (and how many) enter the calculation; how to handle population ethics (Parfit's repugnant conclusion); whether longtermism follows from consequentialism or distorts it.

## Known weaknesses

Aggregation produces troubling implications: the well-being of many small parties can in principle outweigh the catastrophic loss of one (the "transplant surgeon" case; sacrificing one for several). Demandingness: strict consequentialism can demand essentially total devotion to maximizing welfare, leaving no room for personal projects, partial relationships, or supererogation. Calculation problems: real-world consequences are uncertain, intersubjective utility comparisons are contested, and the tradition can lead to overconfident quantitative reasoning about deep uncertainty (a current concern with longtermism in effective altruism). Risks of moral fanaticism when the framework is taken to extreme.

## Primary sources

- Jeremy Bentham, "An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation" (1789)
- John Stuart Mill, "Utilitarianism" (1861)
- Henry Sidgwick, "The Methods of Ethics" (1874)
- Peter Singer, "Practical Ethics" (1979/2011); "Animal Liberation" (1975); "The Most Good You Can Do" (2015)
- Derek Parfit, "Reasons and Persons" (1984); "On What Matters" (2011/2017)
- Toby Ord, "The Precipice" (2020) – longtermist application
- William MacAskill, "What We Owe the Future" (2022) – longtermism in popular form

## Especially useful for

Policy analysis, cost-benefit reasoning, comparing interventions, animal welfare, global health and development, prioritization under resource constraints, situations where the question is genuinely "what produces the best outcome" rather than "what is owed".

## Less useful for

Situations where rights-violations or dignity-violations are the stakes and aggregation would license them; ethical questions where the welfare framework is itself the contested issue; situations of deep value-pluralism where there is no shared welfare function to maximize.
