# Ecomodernism

## What this tradition sees

Ecological problems as soluble through *intensified* rather than reduced human technology, urbanization, and decoupling – using land, energy, and resources more efficiently so that human flourishing can expand while environmental impact contracts. The tradition is the strongest opposition within environmentalism to degrowth, deep ecology, and most ecofeminist positions.

## Core concepts

- **Decoupling** – the empirical and normative claim that economic and human welfare growth can be separated from environmental impact through technology and efficiency
- **Land sparing** (vs. land sharing) – the strategy of intensifying productive uses on small footprints to leave large areas to nature, rather than diffusing low-impact activity across landscapes
- **The good Anthropocene** – the framing that human transformation of the planet is unavoidable and the project is to do it well rather than to undo it
- **Nuclear, GMO, intensive agriculture** – three signature commitments of mainstream ecomodernism that distinguish it from green-left environmentalism
- **Modernization as ecological strategy** – urbanization, electrification, and dematerialization treated as environmentally beneficial, not as the problem

## Key questions

- What is the actual environmental footprint per unit of welfare provided, and how could it be reduced?
- Where does intensification (more output per unit of land/energy) win over diffusion?
- Are the "natural" alternatives being proposed actually lower-impact, or does land-area cost flip the comparison?
- What technologies are being ruled out a priori on aesthetic or ideological rather than empirical grounds?
- Is the framing assuming human welfare must reduce, when the actual constraint is technological choice?

## Characteristic moves

1. Run the numbers on land, energy, and material throughput per unit of welfare delivered.
2. Compare technologies on outcomes rather than associations.
3. Distinguish *symbol* from *substance* – question whether "natural" alternatives are actually better when fully accounted.
4. Defend technology, urbanization, and efficiency against the charge that they are the cause of the ecological crisis rather than the resolution.

## Internal contestation

Internal divide between centrist-liberal ecomodernists (the Breakthrough Institute axis) and more left-leaning eco-pragmatists who share the technology-and-modernization commitment but reject the political-economic conclusions of the centrist current. Debate over how much of the Ecomodernist Manifesto is empirical claim versus political positioning. Tension over nuclear specifically: most ecomodernists pro-nuclear, but the tradition is broader than that single issue.

## Known weaknesses

Track record on decoupling is contested empirically – relative decoupling (efficiency gains) is well-established; absolute decoupling at the scale required is fiercely debated, with degrowth critics having strong evidence against it. The tradition can substitute techno-optimism for political analysis – assuming that the technologies exist or will exist, and the only question is to deploy them, while underanalyzing why deployment lags. Often blind to distributive questions: who benefits from intensification, who pays.

## Primary sources

- "An Ecomodernist Manifesto" (2015) – Asafu-Adjaye, Blomqvist, Brand, Brook, DeFries, Ellis, Foreman, Keith, Lewis, Lynas, Nordhaus, Pielke, Pritzker, Roy, Sagoff, Shellenberger, Stone, Teague
- Ted Nordhaus & Michael Shellenberger, "Break Through: From the Death of Environmentalism to the Politics of Possibility" (2007)
- Stewart Brand, "Whole Earth Discipline" (2009)
- The Breakthrough Institute's ongoing output (breakthroughjournal.org)

## Especially useful for

Energy policy, agricultural productivity questions, urbanization debates, technology assessment in environmental contexts, situations where green-left consensus has hardened into rejection of technologies that may be necessary.

## Less useful for

Distributive and justice questions, situations where the constraint is political-economic rather than technical, value questions about what kind of human relationship to nature is desirable in the first place (here ecofeminism, deep ecology, and decolonial critiques cut deeper).
