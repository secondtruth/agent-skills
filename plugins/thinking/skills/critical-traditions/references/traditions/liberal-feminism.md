# Liberal Feminism

## What this tradition sees

Gender injustice as the unfinished business of liberal universalism: where formal equality has been promised but not delivered, where legal and institutional structures still embed historical exclusions, and where women's autonomy and full personhood are practically constrained even when nominally recognized.

## Core concepts

- **Formal equality** – equal treatment under law, equal access to opportunity, removal of legal discrimination
- **Substantive equality** – the more demanding claim that equal opportunity requires addressing background conditions that make formal equality hollow
- **Autonomy** – the capacity to author one's own life, treated as the central good
- **Capabilities approach** (Nussbaum) – the list of human capabilities that any just society must enable for all, including women
- **Public / private boundary** – examined critically: the "private sphere" has historically been where gendered injustice was protected from public scrutiny

## Key questions

- Where are women excluded from full participation in public life, work, or political agency – formally or effectively?
- Where do formally equal rules produce unequal outcomes because background conditions differ?
- What capabilities are being constrained, and what would expanding them require?
- Is the autonomy framing serving everyone, or is it presuming a particular (often middle-class, often Western) form of self-direction?

## Characteristic moves

1. Identify the formal-vs-effective gap: where the law says one thing and outcomes show another.
2. Trace the institutional design choices that produce that gap.
3. Propose reformist interventions – legislation, anti-discrimination enforcement, institutional redesign, expanded access.
4. Defend the universalist frame against the charge that it is culturally particular.

## Internal contestation

Sharp internal debate over how much can be achieved within existing institutions before liberal feminism collapses into either complicity with the status quo or radicalism by another name. Capabilities-approach feminists (Nussbaum, Sen) argue for a thicker substantive account; classical liberal feminists worry that this drifts into paternalism. Debate over the public/private boundary – how much to politicize the household.

## Known weaknesses

Often charged (from materialist, intersectional, decolonial sides) with mistaking the experience of professional middle-class Western women for the experience of women generally. Its solutions are reformist – it does not have strong tools for situations where the entire institutional structure is the problem. Can underplay structural-economic causes of gendered disadvantage.

## Primary sources

- Mary Wollstonecraft, "A Vindication of the Rights of Woman" (1792) – foundational
- John Stuart Mill, "The Subjection of Women" (1869) – the male liberal canon entry
- Susan Moller Okin, "Justice, Gender, and the Family" (1989)
- Martha Nussbaum, "Women and Human Development" (2000); "Creating Capabilities" (2011)

## Especially useful for

Policy, law, institutional design, formal-equality questions, professional/workplace contexts, anti-discrimination analysis.

## Less useful for

Questions where the structural background (capitalism, colonialism, patriarchy as material system) is the actual driver and reformist intervention would be cosmetic. Critics from the left routinely apply this critique – which is fair, and applies here too.
