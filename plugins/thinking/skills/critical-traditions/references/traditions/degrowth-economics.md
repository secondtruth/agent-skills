# Degrowth Economics

## What this tradition sees

Economic growth as the wrong organizing principle for an economy on a finite planet, and the assumption that growth equals well-being as a historical artifact rather than a fact. Degrowth proposes a planned reduction of material and energy throughput in wealthy economies, coupled with redistribution and reorientation toward well-being, sufficiency, and ecological replenishment.

## Core concepts

- **Throughput** – material and energy flowing through the economy; the variable degrowth proposes to reduce
- **Biophysical limits** – the planetary boundaries within which the economy must operate; treated as binding constraints, not externalities
- **Decoupling skepticism** – the empirical claim that growth has not been, and cannot be, decoupled from environmental impact at the scale and pace required
- **Conviviality** (Illich) – tools and institutions that enable autonomy and meaningful activity rather than maximizing output
- **Decommodification** – removing essential goods (housing, healthcare, care, mobility, education) from market logic
- **Sufficiency** – the principle of "enough" replacing the principle of "more"

## Key questions

- What is the throughput cost of this situation, and what would reducing it require?
- Is "growth" or "scaling" being invoked unreflectively – why is it the goal?
- What is being commodified that could be decommodified, and vice versa?
- What forms of provisioning could meet the underlying needs with less throughput?
- Whose growth is at stake – the Global North's growth in absolute terms, or genuine development for the Global South?

## Characteristic moves

1. Distinguish material throughput from monetary GDP; the two can move independently.
2. Identify which activities provide well-being per unit of throughput (often: care, culture, public goods) and which are throughput-heavy with marginal well-being gain.
3. Propose decommodification of essentials and reduction of luxury-throughput sectors.
4. Locate the *political* obstacles (growth dependency of welfare states, debt-based monetary systems) as central, not peripheral.

## Internal contestation

Active debate over whether degrowth applies globally or only to the Global North (most degrowth scholars now hold the latter explicitly). Disagreement over relationship with green growth / post-growth / steady-state economics – degrowth distinguishes itself sharply from green growth but more sympathetically from steady-state. Internal split over whether degrowth is reformist (policy package: working-time reduction, job guarantee, universal services) or revolutionary (incompatible with capitalism, requires systemic transformation).

## Known weaknesses

The political pathway from growth-dependent welfare states to a degrowth economy is underspecified – the transition risks impoverishing the people degrowth claims to serve. Communications challenge: "degrowth" is rhetorically vulnerable in ways that "ecomodernism" or "green growth" are not. Critics point to weak engagement with technological change, productivity dynamics, and demographic structure.

## Primary sources

- Serge Latouche, "Farewell to Growth" (2009)
- Giorgos Kallis, "Degrowth" (2018); "In Defense of Degrowth" (2017)
- Jason Hickel, "Less is More: How Degrowth Will Save the World" (2020)
- Matthias Schmelzer, Andrea Vetter, Aaron Vansintjan, "The Future is Degrowth" (2022) – current synthesis
- Ivan Illich, "Tools for Conviviality" (1973) – philosophical ancestor

## Especially useful for

Climate policy, post-growth economic design, work and working-time politics, consumption questions, North-South redistribution debates, sufficiency-based design.

## Less useful for

Contexts where the political conditions for any planned economic transformation are absent and the analysis becomes utopian without traction – then it's a critique without an addressee.
